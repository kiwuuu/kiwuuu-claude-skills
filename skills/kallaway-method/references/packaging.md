# Packaging — thumbnails, titles, covers

> **Short form? Read §0 first.** Sections 1–5 below are **YouTube doctrine** — they assume a
> title and a thumbnail consumed side by side, which short form does not have. The mechanics
> transfer; the surfaces don't.

---

## 0. Short-form packaging — the two surfaces

On TikTok / Reels / Shorts there is no title beside the video. Packaging is split across two
surfaces that do **different jobs at different times**:

**A. In-video title text — does the scroll-stopping, at 0:00.**
- 3–5 words, big and bold, on screen in the first second.
- **Never spoken aloud.** If you say it too, you've spent a surface saying one thing twice.
- Carries the *angle* the spoken line doesn't. Align on **meaning**, differ on **wording**:
  spoken *"your AI replies in ten seconds"* + text **"YOUR AI REPLIES TOO WELL"**.
- This is where the desire loop lives in short form. Craft detail: [hooks.md §1 and §9](hooks.md).

**B. Grid cover — does the bingeability work, later.**
- Browsed *after* the fact, when someone lands on the profile because one video worked.
- Its only job: make the grid scannable, so a visitor sees which *other* videos they want.
- Clear text naming what that video is. This is the mechanical half of stacking content
  minutes; the other half is series numbering ([ideas.md §10](ideas.md)).

**What carries over from the sections below:** the three-step click psychology (§1), the seven
stun-gun elements (§2), the desire-vs-interest loop (§3), the nine tactics (§5), **§6 (Instagram
grid covers — read this one, it's the detail behind surface B above)**, and §7 (catalog value,
which covers short-form half-life explicitly).

**What does not carry over:** §4 (the seven-thumbnail A/B/C testing playbook — no equivalent
tooling), and anything assuming a title and thumbnail are read together. The "thumbnail must not
repeat the title" rule becomes *title text must not repeat the spoken line*.

## §1–§5 and §8 are YouTube-shaped; §6–§7 apply everywhere

Packaging comes **first**, before the script. If it can't win the click, the script is wasted
work. On YouTube he treats it as the most important part of the video, because *"if they don't
click, they don't watch."*

His proof stake: **the same video capped at 1,700 views on one channel and hit 1.2M on another
— one thumbnail change.**

---

## 1. The click decision — three steps, under two seconds

1. **Visual stun gun.** Scrolling the feed, something visual freezes the passive scanning brain
   and flips it into active target-lock. The image alone piques curiosity but does **not** get
   the click.
2. **Title value hunting.** Attention drops to the title, because words are more specific than
   images. The viewer hunts for the value they'd get. *This is why title + thumbnail are one
   unit — you cannot win with only one.*
3. **Visual validation.** They go **back up** to the thumbnail, now anchored by the title's
   promise, to check the elements support it. If they desire the value and trust you'll deliver
   it, they click.

**The three failure modes — use these as your audit:**

| Failure | Symptom | Cause |
|---|---|---|
| **Failure to stun** | Nobody looks | Easy to understand but doesn't pop |
| **Failure to promise** | Looked at, not clicked | Weak or misaligned title |
| **Failure to confirm** | Confusion, no click | Strong title, but the image doesn't support it |

> "This whole thumbnail game is just a cat-and-mouse game of desire and trust."

---

## 2. The seven visual stun-gun elements

1. **Colour science / contrast** — vivid colours within the frame, *and* contrast against your
   whole category (he runs light thumbnails where competitors go dark)
2. **Large face** — recognisable, or an unknown face with a recognisable emotion (usually shock)
3. **Visually compelling graphic**
4. **Large text, big numbers, or dollar amounts** — brains are magnets for words, especially
   big round numbers
5. **Red circles or red arrows** — literally aim attention
6. **Aesthetic imagery** — cinematic, symmetrical, balanced
7. **Design collage** — words and numbers arranged around the subject

> **Maximum three elements per thumbnail.** They're tiny, especially on mobile — too many small
> elements can't be comprehended. His usual trio: himself + text + one graphic.

---

## 3. Titles — the perceived value loop

A great title gives clarity on the value they get if they click. Two flavours:

- **Desire loop** (educational) — words trigger a pain point blocking something they want. The
  three core educational desires: **making money, saving time, getting healthy.**
- **Interest loop** (entertainment) — words trigger curiosity around an unanswered question.

**Construction:** reference the pain point, suggest your solution, name the desired outcome.

**Then the thumbnail must NOT repeat the title.** It's a second surface for trust and clarity,
not an echo.

> Worked example — title *"How to Write a Killer Script That Keeps Viewers Hooked"* (150K
> views). Desire = viewers watching longer, triggered by "keeps viewers hooked". Pain = writing
> bad scripts, answered by "killer script". The **thumbnail** added a squiggly storyline graphic
> plus the words **"basically cheating"** — describing the *silent feeling*, not the redundant
> "scriptwriting masterclass".

**Thumbnail text describes the feeling or the action, never the content.**

## 4. The five-step thumbnail playbook

1. **Approach** — treat it as the most important part of the video. Outsource to a professional
   as soon as you're serious.
2. **Define the loop** — before designing anything, name the desire or interest loop, then the
   specific pain and the specific solution for *this* video.
3. **Concepting (measure twice, cut once)** — choose elements first (list candidate graphics and
   text phrases so you have options), then sketch the composition. Three composition types:
   **symmetrical** (subject centred), **asymmetrical** (rule of thirds), **A/B split screen**
   (a transformation).
4. **Design** — high-quality elements, readable text, well-spaced, brightened. If using a
   graphic it must immediately represent the transformation, via one of four routes:
   (a) show the end state, (b) show the process, (c) show before-and-after, (d) show the
   anti-transformation / pain state.
5. **Testing** — **never ship a single thumbnail.** He designs **7** and A/B/C-tests the best
   **3**. "Pick three options and let the market decide."

---

## 5. Nine non-obvious tactics

1. **Green = good, red = bad.** No other colour pair carries automatic comprehension.
2. **Never put anything in the bottom-right corner** — the timestamp overlay covers it.
3. **Do one photoshoot, ever.** 4K stills or 4K60 video, 5–10 minutes of varied emotions (happy,
   sad, surprised, shocked) and poses (pointing up/down each hand, open hand, closed hand). Hand
   the file to your designer and never shoot again.
4. **Face emotion skews the perceived promise** — match it deliberately to the emotion the
   viewer should feel.
5. **No small elements.** Imagine it at 1/16th of an iPhone screen. Max three elements.
6. **Use shadows/shading** to separate subject from background and text.
7. **Bait the click without shame.** It's only clickbait if the content doesn't match the
   promise. If the content delivers, baiting the click is required.
8. **Keep a swipe file** — screenshot every interesting thumbnail you see, share it with your
   designer.
9. **Never rush the thumbnail last.** Start the process *with* it.

**On faces:** use a celebrity's face when the video is about them — known faces carry attached
meaning, so comprehension is instant. But if nobody knows you, **no face may beat your face** —
an unknown face can break trust. Never use a face that doesn't represent the desire loop.

## 6. The Instagram cover cheat code

Instagram covers do a job YouTube thumbnails don't: they make the **profile grid browsable**.
When someone lands on your profile after one good video, the covers are what convert one view
into a session. Give every cover clear text saying what that video is, so a visitor scanning
the grid immediately sees which others they'd want.

This is the mechanical half of bingeability — the other half is series numbering
([ideas.md](ideas.md) §10).

## 7. Catalog value — packaging as a long-term asset

Audience size ≠ asset value. **Catalog value** is the ability of already-published content to
keep generating rewatches long after its distribution window closed. Old videos are ongoing
sales agents.

**Content half-life by platform:**

| Platform | Half-life |
|---|---|
| X | ~48 hours |
| Short-form channels | ~5 days |
| YouTube | ~3–4 weeks |

**Nine tactics to build it:** evergreen topics over time-sensitive · build linked series · email
your list to drive traffic *backward* into old content · Story Highlights to surface the archive
· Instagram Repost at 2–3 months · cover text optimised for grid scanning · thematic highlights
by topic · repost top performers on a **90-day cycle** · a CTA in every video.

> Cited numbers on his own marketing account: 60,000+ followers from ~5M views and 20,000+ email
> conversions in 90 days — **with zero videos above 1M views.** Catalog and conversion, not
> virality.

---

## 8. Ordering rule

**Packaging → outline → script.** Always. And on YouTube specifically: title and thumbnail are
consumed simultaneously, so design them as one unit, never sequentially.

## 9. Captions — the post copy under the video

Distinct from on-screen captions (those are in [hooks.md §1](hooks.md)). This is the text
attached to the post.

**What the corpus shows.** His captions are almost always **the spoken hook, restated verbatim
or near-verbatim**, followed by 4–6 niche hashtags:

> Caption: *"Here's how to write a hook so good it's scientifically impossible to skip
> #storytelling #content #marketing #socialmedia #video"*
> Spoken line 1: *"Here's how to write a hook so good it is scientifically impossible to skip."*

**Why it works, mechanically:** the platform transcribes the video and builds its topic map
largely from the transcript ([ops.md §2](ops.md)) — the caption reinforces the same keywords,
and a viewer who reads the caption before the audio loads gets the topic clarity anyway.

**Rules:**
- **Restate the hook, don't tease it.** The caption is a second topic-clarity surface, not a
  place to be clever. Nothing is gained by withholding.
- **4–6 hashtags, niche not broad.** He runs `#storytelling #content #marketing #socialmedia
  #video` — category tags, not reach tags.
- **Hashtags are icing.** The transcript is the ranking signal; the tags are a small assist.
- **Where the CTA lives is the video, not the caption.** In his corpus the ask is spoken, not
  written in the post copy.
- **Kiwuuu house style overrides tone here** — one lowercase confident line, per
  `kiwuuu-carousel`. Take the *structure* from this section, the *voice* from
  `kiwuuu-brand-voice`.
