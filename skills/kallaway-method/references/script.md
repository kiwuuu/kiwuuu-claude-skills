# Script — writing the thing, in his order

He writes **every** script in this exact order, and the order is the point.

```
1. PACKAGING   (title / thumbnail / cover)
2. OUTLINE     (body points — with a hard gate)
3. INTRO       (the hook)
4. BODY        (2-1-3-4)
5. OUTRO       (the fortune cookie)
   + CTA       (native embed)
```

**Why outline comes before intro:** anybody can learn to write a compelling intro — it's "just
a few Lego bricks" and once you know the formula you can print them on demand. The real moat is
having something genuinely non-obvious to say in the **body**. So you validate the body first.

> "Idea, title and outline are all finished before breaking ground on the script itself — the
> measure twice, cut once of content."

---

## Step 1 — Packaging first

Covered fully in [packaging.md](packaging.md). The rule that matters here: if the packaging
can't win the click, writing the script is wasted work. Kill the idea instead.

---

## Step 2 — The outline, and the hard gate

Put a quick, dirty, **bulleted** outline on the page. Its only purpose is a gut check: do you
actually have something worth saying, or are you about to regurgitate common knowledge?

Uniqueness can come from four places:
1. the tip itself
2. the process of distillation
3. how you say it
4. the example you use to explain it

For each bullet, layer **what / why / how** — what it is, why it matters, how it fits the
larger puzzle. Then zoom out and ask: *is this unique?*

> 🚧 **HARD GATE: if the outline has no unique points, do not write the script.** Go research
> more, watch more source material, find more novel points. He simply doesn't make the video.

This is the highest-leverage rule in the whole method and the one most likely to be skipped.

---

## Step 3 — The intro hook framework (5 parts)

Works in short and long form. Parts 4 and 5 are interchangeable.

1. **Immediate context** — bluntly state what this is about, as fast as possible, so they can
   opt in and the click is confirmed.
2. **State the common belief** — restate the conventional take. Builds common ground *and* sets
   up the contrast. (This is the "direct contrast" that doubles the swing — see hooks.md §3.)
3. **Contrarian take** — contradict it. This stuns them into hearing you out.
4. **Proof** — why they should trust your new approach.
5. **Plan** — an ordered list of what you'll deliver.

Full hook craft: [hooks.md](hooks.md).

---

## Step 4 — The body: the 2-1-3-4 method

**Order your body points by strength: second-best first, best second, then third, then fourth.**

**Why it works:** brains work in patterns. One amazing thing is "cool." Two amazing things
where the second beats the first creates a subconscious pattern of *rising value* — so they
must stay for the third. Lead with your best and point two is a slight drop, and you've taught
them value is diminishing.

**Why point one still has to be strong:** at the end of the intro the viewer compiles what they
already know plus what you promised, and forms an expectation. Your first point is where the
biggest reality-vs-expectation gap gets created. If it repeats something they've heard, they
bounce immediately — the classic behaviour is watching one point, scrolling to the timestamps
hunting for anything unique, finding nothing, leaving.

**The album analogy:** the billion-stream single is almost never track 1 (that's an intro) and
never track 2. Track 2 is great but not the best; the climax lands at track 3 or 4. The rise
from 2→3→4 is what makes people play the whole project.

**Pacing:** constant zoom out / zoom in — big picture, then tactics, big picture, then tactics.
"Kind of like a wave." It adds comprehension and lets people lock into the full story while
still extracting one tactical piece.

**Between every point: a re-hook.** See [story.md](story.md) §2.

---

## Step 5 — The outro (the fortune cookie)

Feels like an afterthought; measurably adds retention, shares, likes and rewatches. Do three
things:
1. Leave them on a high note.
2. Wrap the video cleanly — close the last loop.
3. Summarise the points and remind them of the pain you just solved.

The purpose is to make the lasting impression *"reality beat my expectations."* Likes, comments,
shares and rewatches — everything that powers the algorithm — come from the high note.

> The free chocolate a restaurant brings with the check. It's deliberate: it reminds you of two
> hours of good service at the exact moment you decide how to feel about the meal.

## The CTA — native embeds, never ad breaks

**Core principle:** the goal of content is to hypnotise the viewer into forgetting they're
watching content. Breaking that state for an ad "brings someone back up to the surface — they
realise they're watching a video they don't need to watch, they should be doing work, and they
leave."

- **Non-native ad** = the interrupting break. *"We'll be right back — by the way, if you want
  to buy this couch…"* Worst possible placement is the very beginning.
- **Native embed** = weave the thing into the conversation, or set the conversation up so that
  your resource **is** the answer to the point you just made.

**How to build one:** decide at the **outline stage** which body point naturally suits your
resource as the solve. Then when you reach that point, frame the pain and offer the resource.
Two mechanics: open a loop with a pain point then give the solve; or speedrun a framework so
fast that most people can't absorb it, then offer the resource as the recap.

Requirements: the resource must be **free** and must genuinely solve the pain you just named.
Never break flow — show it natively and move on.

**Placement maths for a real sponsor read:** if your average view duration is 8 minutes, put
the interrupting ad *after* 8 minutes. The brand will want it earlier; 8 minutes is when the
average person leaves, so don't break hypnosis before that point. Your *own* native embeds go
in as early and as often as you can without alienating sponsors.

### The short-form CTA — pick by account, they are not interchangeable

**Media-game channels** (Absurdity, REKT, BLACK BOX, THE MACHINE) — what he does 51 of 53 times:
```
[NAME THE ASSET, WITH A NUMBER IN IT]
+ [ONE LINE ON WHY IT'S VALUABLE / WHO ELSE HAS IT]
+ "comment [ONE-WORD KEYWORD] and I'll send it through, for free"
```
Placed **last**, median 96% through. Verbatim examples in
[../assets/hook-bank.md](../assets/hook-bank.md).

**Anything selling Kiwuuu** — use the offer-led CTA instead:
```
[NAME THE DISQUALIFIER — who this is NOT for, as a question]
+ [THE ONE NUMBER that identifies a real buyer]
+ [ask for that number, or point at the deepest asset]
```
> "Under 50 messages a day? Ignore this. Past 200 and losing the late-night ones? Tell me your
> after-hours volume and I'll tell you what it's costing you."

The keyword version harvests leads into a close that currently converts at zero. Full reasoning
and the metric that replaces comment-counting: [ops.md §8b](ops.md).

## The speedrun: his actual six-step working order

1. **Research** — bullet everything interesting about the topic, scoring each on the shock
   scale. Target output: **5–10 bullets**.
2. **Write the desired emotion at the top of the page** and use it as a filter for everything
   that follows.
3. **Pick the hook structure** — check which formats are working in your niche right now, take
   one, tweak it, verify the four commandments, pair it with a visual.
4. **Pick the story structure** — check what's working in the niche, add your spin, write the
   section headers on the page. Just writing the headers "is very helpful in the flow."
5. **Write the script** — "it's really just about layering the facts in order and adding
   transitions to smooth it out. That's all you have to do to write a banger script."
6. **Read it back with the emotional filter in mind.** Did it drive the intended emotion? If
   not, rewrite for more visceral phrasing.

### The four-question final check (answerable from the text alone)
1. Is this story interesting?
2. Is it as compressed as it can possibly be?
3. Does this hook actually hook me on its own?
4. What emotion do I feel when I finish reading?

> If it passes on text alone, you're good — visuals only make it better.

## The angle and the shock score

**Step 1 — find the angle.** The angle must be exactly one of two things: **shocking**
(entertainment) or **useful** (education). There is no third option.

**Step 2 — the core question:** *what do most people believe that I disagree with?*

**Step 3 — score it.** Rate each fact, take, or implication **0–100** on the shock score:
*"out of a hundred people in a room, how many would have thought that previously?"* Low prior
belief = high shock score.

**Step 4 — the zigzag.** Alternate: give them something they *do* believe (confirm), then
something they don't (shock), in succession down the whole script.

> Prince Charming walks into the woods — the common belief is he keeps walking. A dragon pops
> out: nobody predicted it, high shock score, new stakes, new reality. Then *"most people think
> they're going to fight — and they do"*: a believed beat, given deliberately after a shock.

**On conflict:** it isn't a separate tool. "Conflict is basically a form of contrast."
Conflict applies when there are human subjects; when the subject is a company or a machine, the
mechanism is still contrast.

## The seven winning story structures

Pick the outline before you write. Each is hook → middle → outro; the middle is what varies.

| Structure | Middle | Best for | Pair with hooks |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Breakdown** | context/shock → what happened or how it works → why it matters | Launches, news, new tech, analysis | Secret reveal, question, comparison, contrarian |
| **Newscaster** | full context → *(optional short take)* | Journalism, factual reporting | Secret reveal, question, comparison, problem |
| **Case study explainer** | the framework, 2–4 pieces in succession | Teaching a skill or framework fast | Case study, question, comparison |
| **Listicle** | the list, 3–5 items | Mutually exclusive atomic units | List, problem |
| **Problem solver** | problem (agitate) → solution | Educational and product use cases | Problem, question, contrarian |
| **Tutorial** | step-by-step flow | Explaining a system or process | Education, list |
| **Personal story** | first-person story, start/middle/end | Motivation, inspiration, POV | Personal experience, problem, contrarian |

**Disambiguations:** *Tutorial vs Listicle* — tutorial steps run in succession toward **one**
outcome and can't stand alone; list items each stand alone. *Tutorial vs Case study* — tutorial
is how-to steps; case study is analysis of the pieces that produced the outcome.

**Sequencing shortcut for slotting facts:** imagine telling this to a friend. You know the facts
and you know your hook — what order would make them understand it best? That's your sequence.

## Using AI to write these — his own position

He explicitly **does not** let an AI write scripts end to end: it "feels artificial and doesn't
hit the quality bar." What works is using the system plus automation to stitch a **90% draft as
fast as possible**, then editing manually from there.

**The recursive training loop** (worth copying into our own workflow):
1. Generate or paste a script into a project with stored system instructions.
2. Hand-edit it yourself.
3. Ask the model to analyse the differences between your edit and its original.
4. Tell it to **fold those differences into its system instructions**.

Repeat every video. It's manual fine-tuning. ⚠️ It only works if you already know what a good
script looks like — otherwise you're training it on noise.
