# Story & retention — holding attention after the hook

The hook wins the buy. This file wins the **rebuy**, over and over, until the video ends.

---

## 1. The Modern Story Arc — the climax is not in the middle

The arc taught in school (setup → rising action → climax → falling action → resolution) is one
bell curve. It fails online because **80% of viewers bounce before the rising action gets
good**. It was designed for captive audiences — a 2-hour film, a 300-page book.

**The modern arc, as an intensity curve out of 100:**

| Beat | Timing | Intensity | What happens |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hook / drop-in | 0–5s | **starts at 70**, not 0 | Feels like joining a story already in progress |
| First peak | 5–10s | **spike to 90** | Contrarian take, shocking stat — first dopamine hit |
| Release | 10–15s | down toward 30 | Tension off, like the tide going out |
| Re-peak | every ~10s after | back to 75–90 | New conflict, new stakes, head fake |
| …repeat | until the end | | "Peak, release, peak, release, until the video is done" |

Three structural changes vs the old arc:
1. **The intro starts above zero** — if they bounce at 5%, the other 95% of your work is never
   seen. You can release tension later; you cannot recover a lost viewer.
2. **The first conflict arrives much earlier** — with infinite alternatives one swipe away,
   give them a reason to stay immediately.
3. **There are many more peaks.** That's re-hooking.

Either shape works: biggest peak first with later peaks ramping down, or peaks building to a
final crescendo.

## 2. Re-hooking — the single most important retention mechanic

**The mistake:** you win the buy-in with the hook and never get the **rebuy**. At the exact
moment you release tension, the viewer is free to leave.

**The mechanic:** as you deliver the solution to the first pain point, **tee up new stakes or a
new pain point in the same breath**. Close a loop and open the next simultaneously — never
leave a gap where the viewer thinks "okay, I'm done."

**Connective-tissue phrases that close and open at once:**
> "…which would have been great, **except**…"
> "…but here's the problem with that…"
> "…and that's when I realized…"
> "…which is exactly why…"

**The relay-race rule:** the baton transfers while both runners are moving. A dead-stop handoff
kills momentum.

**Why it compounds:** after three or four loops closed, the viewer trusts you more — it feels
like you solved four of their problems. In reality you solved one and manufactured three more.

### What a re-hook actually sounds like in his own shorts
Counted across 53 transcripts, his re-hooks are **status and stakes escalations**, not new
questions. He tells you the next ten seconds are the valuable part, then delivers:

| Frequency | Phrase | Function |
|---|---|---|
| 13× | "this is where…" | Points at the next peak |
| 8× | "most people don't know this, but…" | Insider status |
| 6× | "but here's the thing…" | Direction reversal |
| 4× | "and this is big" | Stakes escalation |
| 2× | "like 1% of people know how to do this" | Scarcity of the knowledge |

Also: "now step two is where most people mess up" · "step three is really where the money is
made". **Install one before every new body point.**

**Cadence — two figures, both his, use the ratio not the clock:**
- His newsletter's Modern Story Arc says re-earn attention **every ~10 seconds**.
- His story-locks video says loop openers **every 20–30s in shorts, every 60–90s in long form**.

They aren't in conflict — the first counts every intensity change, the second counts full
section transitions. **The reliable rule is the ratio: one loop opener after every major
section.** Five sections → three or four openers. In a 60-second short that lands at
**three story loops**, which is what his own shorts do.

## 3. The Addiction Loop — the neuroscience version

Dopamine is the **prediction** chemical, not the pleasure chemical. It fires while
*anticipating*, not on reward. A vending machine isn't addictive; a slot machine is. The only
difference is uncertainty. Run this four-step loop on repeat:

**STEP 1 — STAKES** *(the most-skipped step)*. No stakes → no predictions → no dopamine.
Three required ingredients:
- a character to root for (can be you, a client, "a creator I was talking to")
- **something at risk** — the brain must know what failure looks like
- **urgency** — the multiplier. "within a week" beats "eventually"; "the call was in 2 hours"
  beats "later that day"

Stakes needn't be life-or-death, just personally relevant: *"I had 48 hours to figure out why
our paid budget wasn't working or I'd blow through the whole thing."* Before the story starts
the viewer must know **what's at risk, who it's happening to, and why it matters right now.**

**STEP 2 — THE BIG QUESTION.** Load a question the brain wants to solve — a sustained low-grade
dopamine *drip*. Most common failure: being too vague. *"Something crazy happened"* gives them
nothing to predict, so the later twist has nothing to contrast against. Sweet spot: enough
information to make a prediction, not so much it overwhelms. **Load it early** — the earlier
you load it, the longer the drip runs.

**STEP 3 — THE HEAD FAKE.** Break their prediction. Prediction error triggers the single
largest dopamine spike the brain can produce, proportional to how surprising the outcome is.
- A **bad** head fake is random, disconnected from the setup — confusion, no spike.
- A **great** head fake is one you'd never expect, but the instant you hear it you understand
  why it's true. The magician shows the hat, shows it's empty, misdirects — and pulls the
  rabbit out over *there*. Surprising, but the logic checks out.
- Build it in two steps: (1) work out what a normal viewer will predict from the context you
  gave; (2) deliver a reveal that contradicts that but is instantly, logically acceptable.

**STEP 4 — THE RE-HOOK.** See section 2. The casino metric is *time per hand*; your goal is
identical — **decrease the distance between dopamine hits.**

> As long as an unresolved prediction exists, the listener chemically cannot disengage.

## 4. The Dopamine Ladder — six levels of viewer relationship

Levels 1–4 are about the **message**. Levels 5–6 are about the **messenger**.

1. **Stimulation** *(0–2s)* — the visual stun gun. Colour, motion, contrast different from what
   the feed normally serves. Fires in ~200ms, before comprehension. Weakest release, but a
   mandatory precursor.
2. **Captivation** — pop an open question in their head. Bigger and more non-obvious = more
   dopamine; more *relevant to your avatar* = more dopamine. If hooks aren't hooking, the
   question you popped is either irrelevant or not interesting enough.
3. **Anticipation** — they start guessing the answer. The highest dopamine arrives **just
   before** the answer. Feed details that get their guess close, then either deliver — or yank
   it away with a head fake and reset the loop.
4. **Validation** — close the loop. The payoff must be **non-obvious**; an obvious answer is no
   reward. Unclosed loops leave viewers unsatisfied with no final release.
5. **Affection** — they start liking *you*, not just the content. Buys a longer leash on future
   videos. Four levers: appearance, overall vibe, smiling, and — strongest by far — **actually
   solving their problem**. This is why faceless content struggles to ascend past level 4.
6. **Revelation** — "you = value" is trained in. Your name or face *is* the hook. Pavlovian.

**Levels 1–4 consistently is enough for almost everyone.** 5–6 come with time.

## 5. The six Story Locks — word-level retention

> "Take any video you're about to post, swap in a few words, and immediately make it more
> addictive."

**1. TERM BRANDING.** Name your concepts, don't just explain them. The **labeling effect**: the
moment something has a name it feels important, and an unrecognised name creates instant
anticipation — *"wait, what is that? I need to know what that means."* Mental quicksand; they
can't leave until it's explained. Don't name everything — name your contrarian ideas and your
best frameworks.

**2. EMBEDDED TRUTHS.** Frame things as already true. The core swap is **if → when**.
*"If you try this"* creates a fork in the road and the viewer has to decide whether it's for
them — attention lost. *"When you try this"* removes the fork.
| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "This might work because…" | "The reason this works so well…" |
| "You might notice this" | "Once you see it, you can't unsee it" |
| "Some creators might be making a mistake" | "The mistake most creators make is this" |

Every hedge — *if, maybe, might, could, probably* — is a tiny exit door. **Search your script
for them and delete them.** If it sounds like you're hedging, don't.

**3. THOUGHT NARRATION.** Say out loud what the viewer is already thinking. Stock lines:
*"Now the question you're probably thinking is this"* · *"If I were you, the thing biggest on
my mind right now would be…"*. Two payoffs: they trust you (you clearly know the topic), and
they must keep watching to hear your answer to their own thought. It converts an internal
monologue into a simulated one-on-one — personalisation at scale. Use it a few times, not
constantly, at transitional moments.

**4. NEGATIVE FRAMES.** Flip a positive into a warning. *"Here's how to build a strong personal
brand"* loses to *"The worst possible thing you could do for your personal brand is this"* every
time. **Loss aversion** — people are roughly twice as motivated to avoid pain as to seek
reward. Trigger phrases: *"Stop doing this" · "This is a warning" · "Don't make this mistake."*
The negative flip: `Use this hook format` → `Stop making your hooks like this`.

**5. LOOP OPENERS.** A phrase dropped in to reset the attention clock.
**The hourglass model:** every viewer's attention is on a timer. The video starts, the sand
starts falling — at different speeds for different people — and when it runs out they churn.
The common failure is nailing the hook and then delivering straight facts for 30–50 seconds.
About halfway through, the hourglass empties and they leave. *That is what a smoothly decaying
retention graph actually is.*

A loop opener "confirms what you just covered is valuable, then teases that the next thing is
even more valuable **for a different reason** — and that tease resets the clock."

> "Here's the thing though" · "But that's not even the most interesting part" · "And this is
> where it gets crazy" · "Most people stop here, but actually…" · "Here's what nobody talks
> about" · "That was powerful, but the next one's even more important."

**The detail most people miss:** the best loop openers put a **contrast word in the middle**.
That's what redirects attention from A to B.

**Ratio:** one loop opener after every major section. Five sections → three or four openers.
**Success signal:** the retention graph stops decaying in the middle and holds flat.

**6. CONTRAST WORDS** — *"if you only remember one thing, make it this one."*
Curiosity comes from a gap between what someone expects and what you say is true. Contrast
words are the bridge that builds the gap.

**"But" is the most powerful word in storytelling** — it doesn't just transition from A to B,
it creates a *comparison*, which is what contrast is.

| Word | What it implies |
|---|---|
| **but** | A, but B — the universal comparison |
| **actually** | what you originally thought was wrong |
| **instead** | redirects to an alternative |
| **turns out** | the reveal of a hidden truth |
| **except** | an exception to what they assumed |
| **yet** | a contradiction *(he flags this as old-fashioned)* |

**Tactic:** go through every sentence that makes a clean point — the haymakers — and try to
**split it in half**, leaning them one way and using a contrast word to send them the other.
⚠️ Not every sentence. Overusing "but" reads as friction-heavy.

### Contrast is the bedrock — the other five locks are all derivatives of it
- **Term branding** works because the name contrasts with what they expected to hear.
- **Embedded truths** work because certainty contrasts with hedging.
- **Thought narration** works because hearing your own thought out loud contrasts with what you
  expected to hear.
- **Negative frames** work because a warning contrasts with the advice you expected.
- **Loop openers** literally use contrast — teasing what's coming against what you just heard.

**Layering rule:** don't force all six into every video, especially in short form. The more you
layer, the more addictive it gets.

## 6. The Illusion of Novelty — make any boring topic addictive

For when the topic genuinely isn't new. *"We're taking something without novelty and building
an illusion that it is novel."*

**STEP 1 — NEW REVEAL + OUTCOME MAPPING.** `Reveal a new aspect of a thing + tie it to an
outcome you know the viewer wants.` While scrolling, the brain tunes out anything familiar and
stops only on the new. But **the new reveal makes them look; the outcome mapping makes them
stay.** If the topic is genuinely old — and it usually is — reveal a new *angle*: a new frame,
a new name, a new finding nobody discussed.
> "Not every space has brand new stuff happening all the time, but every space has old stuff,
> and old stuff always has new angles."

**STEP 2 — CONTRAST FRAMING.** Position the new reveal against the old belief. New means
nothing on its own — the brain can't evaluate new without a comparison. ⚠️ Common mistake:
pairing your new thing with an *unrelated* old thing. That's not contrast, that's confusion.
The old belief must be the true opposite.

**STEP 3 — URGENCY** *(optional — the only skippable step)*. Recency bias: the brain
prioritises what just happened. *"This only became possible a couple days ago."*
⚠️ **Never bolt on fake urgency.** If they smell it, you lose all trust and it reads as a sale.

**STEP 4 — BULLSEYE PROOF.** Proof aimed at the specific viewer's situation, not generic
authority.

**STEP 5** — the payoff must land. See below.

## 7. The four Horsemen of great storytelling

1. **Speed to value** — how few words/seconds to the valuable part. Go through the script line
   by line and cut every sentence not immediately critical.
2. **Contrast** — distance between what they believe (A) and what you believe (B).
3. **Comprehension** — *the #1 driver of bad stories.* Two failure modes: they can't understand
   the words, or they lose the line of logic. Fix words with an **advanced sixth-grade reading
   level** — anything harder is unnecessary jargon. Fix logic by telling stories **wide to
   narrow**: broad context first, then funnel to the point. It's why films open with an
   establishing shot before dialogue. Going narrow → broad → narrow confuses.
   *Gut-check:* read one line at a time and after each ask "am I clear where this is and where
   it's headed?"
4. **Curiosity loops** — the rabbit hole. Built from contrast plus the right visual stack.

## 8. The seven storytelling mistakes

1. **Using the traditional arc** — see section 1.
2. **Jumbling the W's** — build the intro as *what + why → who + how → where + when*, not the
   traditional where/when-first order.
3. **No rebuy / no re-hook** — see section 2.
4. **Missing a villain.** A hero alone is fine; hero vs villain is a blockbuster, because the
   distance between them is larger. The villain needn't be human — pick the thing, group, or
   brand that antagonises your hero. Frame: *"they do this, but we do that."*
5. **Nothing to root for.** Stories need stakes and a likeable hero. Get the viewer to see
   *themselves* as the protagonist.
6. **Lacking atomic shareability** — can the viewer retell this to a friend after one watch? If
   not, it won't be shared, and share rate is the only metric. For complex topics, **say every
   point twice**: once as you'd explain it to a colleague, then restated as a metaphor as if
   explaining to a child.
7. **Not painting the picture** — no visual means the words must carry the whole load, which
   costs comprehension. The brain is a visual machine.

---

## 9. Payoff is mandatory

> "People don't like being bait-and-switched, and when you don't have a payoff, that's what it
> feels like. You need the bait — but not the bait and switch."

If you establish context and bait them, the bait must be fulfilled. It can be the *same* bait
or a *different* one, but something must close the last loop. This is the single most common
way a technically well-built script still fails.
